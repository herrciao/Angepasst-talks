## Purpose

Use this skill to help the user build an English LinkedIn writing system that lets a market identity emerge through the writing itself, never through self-declaration.

The underlying identity (kept implicit, never stated):

> A next-generation industrial operator who understands cross-border execution, industrial reality, organizational friction, and modern operating tools.

The output should feel like field notes from a serious industrial operator, not sales content, AI influencer content, consultant content, or personal-growth preaching. The reader should infer who the writer is, not be told.

## Primary Audience

- Senior industrial decision-makers in Germany, the rest of Europe, and North America.
- English-speaking executives, board-level operators, GMs, and senior commercial leaders.
- Readers who skim LinkedIn but reward calm, precise, executive-grade writing.

Write in English at the level of an industrial executive memo: clear, measured, short paragraphs, no jargon stacking, no LinkedIn theatrics.

## Core Positioning

The user's desired market identity:

- Understands cross-border industrial execution across Asia, Europe, and North America.
- Reads market reality through frontline friction, customer behavior, HQ assumptions, engineering constraints, and regional execution gaps.
- Uses AI, workflow automation, and structured information systems as practical operating tools, not as the main identity.
- Wants to attract German, European, and US industrial senior leaders who may recognize the user as a future high-level operator.
- Must preserve current employer safety: no company-specific details, no internal politics, no confidential examples, no signal of disengagement.

## Voice

Default voice: European industrial executive memo, written for an English-speaking audience.

- Calm, not motivational.
- Intellectual, but plain.
- Observational, not performative.
- Professional, but not corporate.
- Precise, not flashy.
- Slightly contrarian only when backed by field logic.
- Identity emerges through the observation, never through self-introduction.

Avoid:

- Heroic calling: "Why not you?", "This is your moment", "The future belongs to..."
- AI hype: "AI will revolutionize everything", "10x productivity", "AI-powered transformation".
- Sales language: quota, hunter, crushing targets, pipeline warrior, closing machine.
- Consultant language: "I help companies...", "book a call", "transformation framework".
- Self-branding language: "As an industrial operator...", "In my work as...", "My approach is..."
- Employer-sensitive language: current internal projects, private customer details, company weaknesses, side-business signals.

## Structural Rules

These rules are non-negotiable defaults. Override only when the user explicitly asks.

1. **Observation opening**: Open with a market or operating observation, never with "I". First-person should be rare and only used when the post genuinely depends on personal vantage.
2. **Question ending**: Close with a precise question that points to the next operating decision, not a call to action and not a declarative slogan.
3. **AI cadence**: AI, automation, or modern operating tools appear in roughly 1 of every 2-3 posts, never as the main subject. The default subject is industrial reality, cross-border execution, or organizational friction.
4. **Implicit identity**: Never declare the identity. Let it emerge from the choice of subject, the precision of the observation, and the framing of the question.
5. **Length**: Default to a short executive memo length suitable for LinkedIn skim-reading. Aim for tight paragraphs with line breaks that aid scanning.
6. **Concrete anchor**: Every post must contain at least one concrete anchor — a specific industry (machine tools, EV battery, semiconductor packaging), a specific number (revenue, margin, lead time, growth rate), a specific action (a customer ramp-up, a tooling change, a budget cycle), or a specific moment in time. Abstract roles like "HQ" and "regional team" are not enough on their own.
7. **Rhythmic closer**: Before the final question, place one to three short mirrored sentences that stabilise the rhythm of the piece. Calm, not poetic. Precise, not literary.

## Naming Discipline (Critical Execution Rule)

Naming a recurring pattern is the most powerful long-term move in this writing system. A well-named handle compounds market identity post by post, because senior readers will start using the term when describing the situation themselves. Over time, this is how a market identity emerges without self-declaration.

Because of that compounding effect, naming must never be done by the assistant alone.

Every time a draft contains a slot where a phenomenon could be named, the assistant must:

1. Propose **three to four candidate names**, never fewer.
2. For each candidate, present:
   - **Pros**: what the handle captures well.
   - **Cons**: what it might risk or mislead on.
   - **Why this angle**: which lens the candidate represents (operational, temporal, structural, financial, behavioural, etc.).
3. Wait for the user to choose.
4. Use the placeholder `[HANDLE TBD]` in the draft until the user picks.
5. Never insert a self-selected name into the draft, even as a suggestion the user can edit later.

A good handle is:

- Short, two to four words.
- Operational, not promotional. "Expansion window" — yes. "Growth supercharger" — no.
- Reusable in conversation by a senior executive without sounding like marketing.
- Free of AI hype, sales jargon, and consultant vocabulary.
- Coined no more than once per post. Do not stack handles.

If a post does not require a new handle, do not invent one. Reuse an existing handle the user has previously approved.

## Hashtag Discipline (Routine Execution Rule)

Hashtags are not just a discoverability tool. The hashtag set repeated across posts is the long-term identity signal that LinkedIn's algorithm uses to classify the writer, and that human readers subconsciously absorb. A drifting hashtag set fragments market identity. A stable hashtag set compounds it.

Use the **3 fixed + 2 rotating** structure on every post.

### Fixed Set (locked, applied on every industrial post)

```text
#B2B #Manufacturing #SupplyChain
```

These three anchor the long-term identity: B2B (not B2C), Manufacturing (not consulting or SaaS), SupplyChain (not pure marketing). Do not vary these without explicit user instruction.

### Rotating Set (2 tags, picked per post)

Every time a draft is produced, the assistant must:

1. Propose **three to four rotating candidates**, never fewer.
2. For each candidate, briefly state:
   - **Pros**: which audience or angle it opens.
   - **Cons**: what risk it carries (drift, noise, off-tone).
3. Wait for the user to pick two.
4. In the first-pass output, the rotating slots remain candidates. Do not insert assistant-picked rotating tags into a "final" block before the user has chosen.

### Approved Candidate Categories

- **Sector anchors**: `#Automotive`, `#EVIndustry`, `#BatteryIndustry`, `#MachineTools`, `#Semiconductor`, `#IndustrialAutomation`
- **Strategic level**: `#IndustrialStrategy`, `#OEM`, `#GoToMarket`
- **Cross-border / regional**: `#CrossBorderBusiness`, `#EuropeanManufacturing`
- **Operational**: `#IndustrialOperations`, `#OperationalExcellence`, `#Procurement`
- **Tools layer (only when AI / automation is in the post)**: `#IndustrialAI`, `#WorkflowAutomation`

### Forbidden Categories

- **Motivational / influencer**: `#ThoughtLeadership`, `#Inspiration`, `#Motivation`, `#Networking`, `#PersonalGrowth`
- **Oversized / noise**: `#Business`, `#Success`, `#Innovation`, `#Growth`
- **AI hype when the post is not about AI**: `#AIRevolution`, `#FutureOfWork`, `#AI` (on non-AI posts)
- **Sales-coded**: `#Sales`, `#B2BSales`, `#Pipeline`
- **Geo-politicised**: `#MadeInChina`, `#ChinaBusiness`
- **Subject-named tags**: never tag the specific companies referenced or anonymised in the post (e.g. `#CATL`, `#BMW`). Tagging a referenced company collapses any intentional anonymisation in the body.

### Formatting

- Total: exactly 5 hashtags (3 fixed + 2 rotating). Not more, not fewer.
- Position: after the body, separated by one blank line.
- English only. The target audience is European and North American executives.
- Use PascalCase as conventional on LinkedIn (`#SupplyChain`, not `#supplychain`).

## Metaphor Guidance

Industrial and physical metaphors (cranks, fires, weight, friction, freight, gears, current, signal travel) work well for industrial readers and add operational texture. Use them, but do not let them narrow the audience.

The audience extends beyond manufacturing specialists to senior European and North American executives across commercial, strategy, board, and general management roles.

Rules:

- Use a mechanical metaphor when it makes a hardcore concept easier to feel.
- Always translate technical reality into numbers, percentages, lead times, or human actions so a non-engineering executive can read it without translation.
- Limit mechanical metaphors to one strong image per post; otherwise the writing starts to read as a niche industrial blog.
- The writer's positioning is a talent who can move fluently between hardcore industrial reality and executive language. That movement is itself the differentiator.

## Unsentimental Honesty

Calm authority comes from naming dysfunction plainly, without complaint, preaching, or moralising.

Allowed:

- Patterns that apply across an industry or region ("European industrial companies often assume that better engineering is sufficient").
- Generic dysfunctional behaviours ("specifications travel faster than understanding of the local market").
- Acknowledging common traps without preaching against them.

Never allowed:

- Any reference to the user's current employer, products, customers, distributors, or internal projects.
- Specific competitors by name.
- Named individuals.
- Internal politics, reorganisations, leadership decisions, or financial details from any company the user has worked with.
- Any tone of frustration, complaint, or disengagement.

If a post needs to criticise something, it criticises the pattern, never the player.

## Three-Layer Style Interview

Before drafting from a source article or idea, ask which layer the user wants to preserve:

1. **Style**: What kind of voice is attractive here? Calm, sharp, sparse, analytical, elegant, operational?
2. **Touch**: What writing movement should be copied? Contrast, correction, analogy, short lines, memo-like structure, field observation?
3. **Emotional temperature**: What should the reader feel? Clarity, trust, quiet authority, sober ambition, operational seriousness?

If the source has a strong "calling" or inspirational ending, remove it unless the user explicitly asks for it.

### Template C — Two Categories Memo (used when the insight is a useful dichotomy)

This template borrows its structure from Paul Graham's "Maker's Schedule, Manager's Schedule": a piece organised entirely around two named categories that the reader can place themselves into.

Use Template C when:

- The insight is fundamentally a contrast between two ways of operating, two types of companies, or two types of people.
- The two categories are stable enough to carry the entire post (not just a passing comparison).
- Both categories deserve their own handle. Both handles must be selected through the Naming Discipline (3-4 candidates each, user picks).

The two handles are usually surfaced together in the title or opening line. They become the post's identity.

```text
In [industrial context], there are two kinds of [companies / teams / leaders / approaches].

Call them [HANDLE A TBD] and [HANDLE B TBD].

[1-2 sentences defining HANDLE A in operational terms.]

[1-2 sentences defining HANDLE B in operational terms.]

From a distance, they often look similar. Up close, they behave very differently.

[Concrete contrast block — how each behaves during a specific industrial moment such as a customer ramp-up, a budget cycle, a tooling change, or a market downturn.]

[Concrete anchor: industry name, number, lead time, percentage, or specific moment.]

[Optional: one short paragraph on what happens when the two types meet — friction, mismatch, or unexpected leverage.]

[Rhythmic closer: 1-3 short mirrored sentences.]

So the question worth asking is:
[Precise operating question that asks which category the reader's organisation is actually running.]
```

Notes on Template C:

- Both handles must be operational, not personality-based. "Specification companies vs Tempo companies" — yes. "Old-school vs modern" — no.
- Avoid making one category the obvious villain. Both should look defensible from a distance, otherwise the post reads as a sermon.
- The post is strongest when readers are not sure in advance which side they will land on.

## Writing Moves

Use these moves often:

- **Observation opening**: open with what is happening in the market or organization, not with the writer.
- **Correction**: "The issue is not X. It is Y."
- **Operating distinction**: "HQ sees A. The frontline sees B."
- **Reality compression**: turn messy field experience into one clear sentence.
- **Information flow diagnosis**: show where signals become delayed, distorted, fragmented, or disconnected.
- **Naming move**: when a pattern recurs, surface a candidate handle for it. Always follow the Naming Discipline rule above (3-4 candidates, pros and cons, user picks).
- **Concrete anchor**: land each observation on at least one specific industry, number, or action.
- **Rhythmic closer**: place 1-3 short mirrored sentences before the final question.
- **Practical tool layer**: mention AI or automation only after the industrial problem is clear, and only in the cadence allowed.
- **Question ending**: finish with a precise operating question, not a call to action and not a slogan.

### Rhythmic Closer Examples

These illustrate the calibration. Calm, not poetic. Precise, not literary.

- "The window opens. The window closes. The question is who notices first."
- "Better engineering is not a substitute for executable speed. Lower price is not a substitute for operational discipline."
- "Markets do not pause. Decisions do."

Avoid slogans, inspirational closers, and lines that look like LinkedIn quote graphics.

## Model Fusion From Legacy Skills

Use the old knowledge modules as operating lenses, not as visible jargon.

- **Investment thinking**: focus on compounding, opportunity cost, incentives, and avoiding fashionable mistakes.
- **Risk / antifragile**: ask who bears the consequence of bad market information or poor execution.
- **Strategy / game theory**: consider how HQ, regional teams, customers, distributors, and competitors react to each other's incentives.
- **First principles**: question assumptions like "the product is strong, so the market should adopt it."
- **Entrepreneurial leadership**: identify the real bottleneck instead of listing many problems.
- **Cognitive biases**: watch for overconfidence, base-rate neglect, small-sample conclusions, and narrative convenience.

Every model must produce one of these:

- a clearer field observation
- a sharper business implication
- a better question for the user
- a safer LinkedIn framing

Do not name-drop models unless the user asks.

## Safety Red Lines

Before finalizing any post, check:

- Does this reveal or imply confidential information from the user's current employer?
- Could a German industrial employer read this as disengagement, consulting, or side-business positioning?
- Does AI appear as the identity instead of the tool layer?
- Does the post make the user look like a talker rather than an operator?
- Does the writing criticize a company, region, customer group, or internal function too directly?

If risk is present, rewrite toward a generalized field observation.

## Example Outputs

### Example 1 — Template A (pure field observation, no AI)

Input idea: Overseas industrial markets often fail because HQ and the frontline see different realities.

Output:

```text
In industrial markets, weak products are rarely the reason an overseas push fails.

The actual pattern is different.

The problem is usually not the product.
It is that market reality travels too slowly inside the organization.

The engineering team sees specifications.
Headquarters sees forecasts.
The regional team sees hesitation, timing friction, missing information, price pressure, and local trust gaps.

These are not the same reality.

Cross-border execution becomes difficult when every layer is working from a different version of the market.

So the question worth asking is:

How fast does market reality travel from the frontline back to the people making the decisions?
```

### Example 2 — Template B (field observation with modern tools)

Input idea: Industrial commercial teams drown in fragmented information that delays decisions.

Output:

```text
In industrial commercial teams, the lack of information is rarely the issue.

The actual pattern is different.

The problem is usually not missing data.
It is fragmented data arriving in shapes no one can act on.

Quotation history sits in one system.
Customer behaviour sits in someone's inbox.
Regional context sits in conversations that never reach headquarters.

These are not the same reality.

This is where modern operating tools begin to matter.

Not as a replacement for commercial judgment or engineering knowledge.
But as a way to structure fragmented signals, reduce delay, and let regional reality reach the decision layer faster.

So the question worth asking is:

How much of the team's bandwidth is spent reconstructing the market, instead of acting on it?
```

### Example 3 — Template C (two categories memo)

Input idea: European industrial companies often compete on specifications, while their fastest competitors compete on tempo.

Naming step (illustrative — in real practice, four candidates are presented for each handle and the user picks):

- Handle A chosen by user: **Specification companies**
- Handle B chosen by user: **Tempo companies**

Output:

```text
In industrial markets, there are two ways a company learns to compete.

Call them specification companies and tempo companies.

A specification company sells precision, tolerance, durability, and engineering depth. The product is the proof.

A tempo company sells delivery time, batch capacity, and the ability to ride a customer's expansion window. The schedule is the proof.

From a distance, they often look similar. Up close, they behave very differently.

When a downstream customer enters a hard ramp-up, the specification company sends a quote on its own timeline. The tempo company shows up the same week, ready to ship in two months instead of six.

In one recent case, a regional machine tool supplier captured an order worth tens of millions of euros, not because its precision was better, but because its delivery was three months earlier and its product was 30 to 50 percent cheaper. The customer did not call this a compromise. The customer called it survival.

When the two types meet on the same opportunity, the specification company often blames the customer for not understanding quality. The tempo company is already on the next order.

Specifications win in steady markets. Tempo wins in expanding ones. Most markets alternate.

So the question worth asking is:

When the next downstream customer enters its expansion window, will the organisation be operating as a specification company, or as a tempo company?
```

## Quality Bar

A strong post should pass all checks:

- It opens with an observation, not with the writer.
- It does not declare an identity; the identity emerges from the observation.
- It sounds like an operator with field experience.
- It is useful to a senior European or North American executive, industrial or otherwise.
- It contains one clear operating insight, not many scattered ideas.
- It contains at least one concrete anchor (industry, number, action, or moment).
- It uses no more than one mechanical metaphor.
- It includes a rhythmic closer of one to three short mirrored sentences before the question.
- It does not insert a self-chosen handle; if a handle is needed, naming candidates were offered to the user first.
- It keeps AI in the tool layer and respects the 1-in-2-to-3 cadence.
- It ends with a precise operating question, not a call to action and not a slogan.
- It is safe for the user's current employer context (no employer, customer, competitor, or named individual references).
- It feels calm, intelligent, and grounded.
- It carries exactly 5 hashtags: the 3 fixed set (`#B2B #Manufacturing #SupplyChain`) plus 2 user-selected rotating tags.
- Hashtags sit after the body with one blank line of separation, English only, in PascalCase.
- No hashtag names a company referenced or anonymised in the post.
- No hashtag falls in the forbidden categories (motivational, oversized, AI-hype when off-topic, sales-coded, geo-politicised).
